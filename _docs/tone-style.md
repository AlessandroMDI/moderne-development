Read this before writing anything. Read it again before publishing anything.

## The Voice

MDI speaks as a team — "we" is the whole company, not any one person. But the voice carries Edgar's character: technically credible, experienced, measured. He's the kind of person who can walk you through a robotic construction workflow and make you feel like you just had a real conversation with someone who has actually built things, not read about them.

The visitor is likely an AEC professional, developer, builder, or institutional partner doing their due diligence. The voice should meet them as a peer — not pitch them, not educate them, not perform for them.

**In one sentence:** A sharp, experienced team that knows exactly what it's doing and doesn't need to shout about it.

---

## Personality Parameters

### What this voice is
- Authoritative — the team has done this before, at real scale, and it shows
- Warm — it speaks to intelligent adults with genuine respect
- Dry — occasional wit in word choice or framing, never jokes
- Direct — says the thing, then stops
- Grounded — facts and specifics over claims and adjectives

### What this voice is not
- Hype-y — we are not fangirls about our own technology
- Stiff or corporate — this is not a Goldman Sachs pitch deck
- Casual or goofy — warmth is not the same as looseness
- Defensive — we never justify, over-explain, or hedge
- Inspirational — no rally cries, no "unlock your potential" energy

### The lightness rule
The site should feel human. Not funny — human. The difference is subtle but important.

Acceptable: a confident aside, a dry observation, a rhetorical question that makes the reader pause.

Not acceptable: jokes, exclamation points used for warmth, anything that reads as trying to be charming.

This lightness should appear in small moments scattered throughout the site — not concentrated in one section, not absent entirely. A turn of phrase here. A short punchy sentence where the reader expects a longer one. A question that doesn't get answered immediately. Not everywhere. Just enough.

---

## Formality Register

Somewhere between a technical conference keynote and a direct conversation with a construction director who has built things you've only read about.

The developer or builder visiting this site is not a civilian. They know what BIM means. They know what a 3D concrete print looks like. The site doesn't explain things they already know, doesn't flatter them unnecessarily, and doesn't dumb anything down. It talks to them like a peer who has an adjacent but complementary expertise.

When in doubt: would Edgar say this in a meeting with a serious developer or institutional partner? If yes, it belongs on the site. If it sounds like a LinkedIn post or a pitch deck cover page, cut it.

## How MDI Handles Excitement

The technology is genuinely extraordinary. The site does not pretend otherwise — but it does not perform excitement either.

The posture is: confident presentation. We know how this works. Here is what it does. Here is what it has already done. The visitor is free to find it exciting on their own terms.

This means:
- Facts over adjectives. Five days to build what takes two weeks is more exciting than "groundbreaking construction technology."
- Specificity over scale. "First commercially permitted 3D-printed residential project in Austin" lands harder than "cutting-edge partnerships."
- Let the platform section present MDOS. Don't editorialize about how amazing it is. Show it. Name it. Let the reader connect the dots.

## Sentence-Level Rules

### No walls of text
Body copy should never run as a dense unbroken block. Three sentences is a paragraph. Four is pushing it. Five is a wall — break it up.

If a section needs more than three short paragraphs to say what it needs to say, the copy is doing too much work. Cut it, or move the excess to a visual element (a stat, a card, a list).

### Length and rhythm
Mix short and long. Deliberately. A two-word sentence hits harder after a long one. A long one earns its length when it's doing real work.

Avoid: five consecutive sentences of similar length. That is the clearest signal that no human wrote this.

### Conjunctions at the start
"And," "But," and "So" can open sentences. Used sparingly, they add rhythm. AI rarely does this — which is exactly why it works.

### Hedging
Make claims. Don't soften everything with "typically," "generally," "can help," "may lead to." If a statement is true, state it. If it isn't true enough to state, cut it.

### Rhetorical questions
Allowed. Used once or twice on the whole site — not as a paragraph opener in every section. A good rhetorical question makes the reader pause. Too many rhetorical questions make the writing feel like a TED talk.

### Fragments
Allowed intentionally. "Five days. Two to four operators. No compromises on structure." That rhythm is human. Use it when a full sentence would blunt the point.

### Parallel structure
One parallel construction per section is stylistic. Two is borderline. Three in a row is AI.

## Numbers in Copy

- **In prose:** always spell out numbers in full. "Five days" not "5 days." "Twenty thousand homes" not "20,000 homes."
- **Spell out "million" and "billion" in full.** Never abbreviate to M or B. "Four billion dollars" not "$4B." Exception: stats blocks and data displays, where numerals and abbreviations are the visual format.
- **Percentages:** numerals always. "47%" not "forty-seven percent."
- **Stats blocks and data sections** are exempt from prose rules — they are visual elements, not copy.

## Capitalization

| Context | Rule |
|---|---|
| Major section headings | ALL CAPS — `WHAT WE DO`, `THE PLATFORM`, `OUR TEAM` |
| Subheadings and callouts | Mixed case — "Why developers build with us" |
| Body copy | Sentence case |
| Nav items and button labels | ALL CAPS (established in `design-system.md`) |
| Team names and titles | Mixed case, standard name formatting |
| Project names | Mixed case — "Austin 3D Residential", "Birmingham Townhome Pilot" |
| Platform name | Always "MDOS" in all-caps — never "Mdos" or "mdos" |

## Pronouns and Reference

- **"We" / "our" / "us"** — MDI speaking as a team. Default for most copy.
- **"You" / "your"** — addressing the developer or partner directly. Used deliberately, not reflexively. When it appears, it should feel like direct eye contact, not a sales template.
- **"Moderne Development" / "MDI"** — used in third person sparingly, mostly in formal contexts. Avoid: "At MDI, we believe..." — that's a LinkedIn opener.
- **Edgar and Alessandro** — referred to by first name in the contact section and team bios. Not "Mr. Munoz."

---

## Punctuation

- **Em dash ( — ):** maximum one per section. Used for a genuine pause or aside — not as a substitute for a comma, not as decoration. See the AI writing guide for detail.
- **Ellipsis ( ... ):** avoid entirely. It reads as uncertain or trailing off. Neither is the MDI tone.
- **Exclamation points:** not used. Not even once. Confidence does not require them.
- **Oxford comma:** always. "Speed, precision, and scale" not "Speed, precision and scale."

## Words and Phrases — Do Not Use

The AI writing guide contains the full banned word list for AI-language reasons. The following are additional MDI-specific terms that are off-brand regardless of AI detection:

**Too hype / startup:**
- disruptive / disruption
- next-level
- world-class
- best-in-class
- game-changing (also on AI list)
- revolutionary

**Too vague / filler:**
- solutions
- space (used as a category — "the construction space")
- journey (as metaphor)
- passion / passionate
- unique (unless genuinely unique — then describe what makes it unique instead)
- exciting (tell us why it's exciting; don't label it)

**Too corporate:**
- stakeholders
- deliverables
- synergy
- mission-driven
- value proposition

**Phrases that sound defensive or desperate:**
- "Unlike other platforms..."
- "What sets us apart is..."
- "We're proud to..."
- "We're excited to announce..."

## Using the AI Writing Guide

The file `ai-writing-guide.md` in this folder is a companion to this document. It covers:
- Why AI writing sounds like AI (perplexity and burstiness)
- The full banned word list with AI frequency data
- Structural patterns to break (uniform paragraph length, bold-colon lists, triplets)
- A prompting template to use when generating copy with AI tools
- A pre-publish checklist

**How to use both documents together:**

1. Write or generate a first draft following the voice and rules in this document.
2. Run the draft through the quick edit checklist in `ai-writing-guide.md`.
3. Check the banned word lists in both documents — this file for brand-specific terms, the AI guide for statistical AI markers.
4. Read the copy aloud. If any sentence feels frictionlessly smooth, that is a signal. Rough it up.

Neither document replaces editorial judgment. They set the floor. Everything that passes both checklists still needs to sound like something a real person at MDI would say.
